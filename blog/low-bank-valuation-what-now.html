The Valuation Came in Low: What Now?

4 min read
JSC Property Investments

You have signed the contract, your pre-approval is in hand, and the bank’s valuer comes back $40,000 under the price you agreed to pay. It happens more often in a falling market, and the next few days decide whether you settle, renegotiate or walk away.

Why does a low valuation matter?

The bank lends against its own valuation, not the price you agreed. A lower value raises your loan-to-value ratio (LVR) for the same loan, which can trigger lenders mortgage insurance, limit your lender options, or turn into a cash shortfall you have to fund (Vecchio, 2026a).

Here is an example using our own figures. You agree to pay $800,000 and planned to borrow 80%, or $640,000. The bank values the property at $760,000. At 80% of that, the most it will lend is $608,000, leaving you $32,000 short, on top of stamp duty and other purchase costs.

Why are low valuations more likely now?

Valuers price what the evidence supports, and the evidence is softening. In CBRE’s Q3 2026 survey, 56% of its residential valuers reported soft or very limited demand, the first time a majority had reported negatively, and almost two-thirds expected prices to fall over the next year (CBRE, 2026). Recent sales feed directly into valuations, so a falling market makes a gap between contract price and valuation more likely.

What are your options?

1. Get a second valuation. Your broker can request a valuation through a different panel firm or lender. It costs nothing and sometimes resolves the gap outright (Hermosilla, 2026). It is not guaranteed to be higher, and the whole loan must still fit the new lender’s criteria (Vecchio, 2026a).

2. Challenge the valuation with evidence. A challenge works best when it corrects factual errors, such as the wrong land size or bedroom count, and supplies recent settled sales of comparable properties. “Disagreeing with the figure alone is unlikely to change it” (Vecchio, 2026a).

3. Renegotiate the price. A low valuation is useful evidence in a conversation with the seller, especially when your contract is still subject to finance. It is much harder after an unconditional purchase (Hermosilla, 2026).

4. Fund the gap. Add the shortfall from savings. This reduces your buffer and may push your LVR above 80% (Hermosilla, 2026).

5. Use a guarantor. A family member’s equity can cover the gap, but it needs careful structuring (Hermosilla, 2026).

6. Do not proceed. If the contract allows it, walking away may be the right decision. Get legal advice on your rights first (Vecchio, 2026a).

How does the Queensland finance clause work?

Under the standard Queensland contract, a buyer who is subject to finance must apply promptly and pursue approval honestly. Notice that finance has not been obtained must reach the seller by 5pm on the finance date. If you give no notice by then, the seller gains a right to terminate, and an extension needs the seller’s written agreement, which the seller can refuse (Vecchio, 2026b).

In practice, the finance date usually matters more than the size of the gap. Valuations, credit checks and approvals all take time (Vecchio, 2026a). Three rules help:

  • Never waive finance before written, unconditional approval. Once waived, the shortfall is your problem.
  • Ask early whether the valuation is complete. “A pre-approval does not guarantee that the property will be accepted at the purchase price” (Vecchio, 2026a).
  • Act before 5pm. Email sent at or after 5pm can count from the next business day (Vecchio, 2026b).

How do you reduce the risk before you sign?

  • Know the comparable sales. If you cannot justify the price with recent settled sales, a valuer probably cannot either.
  • Set a realistic finance date. In a soft market, 14 days can be tight if a second valuation is needed.
  • Keep a cash buffer. A few percent of the price in reserve can turn a shortfall from a crisis into an inconvenience.
  • Be careful at auction. Auction buyers typically buy unconditionally, so a low valuation becomes your problem alone (Hermosilla, 2026).

Frequently asked questions

What happens if the bank valuation is lower than the purchase price? The bank lends against the lower figure, so you need to cover the difference, renegotiate, get another valuation or, if your contract allows, terminate.

Can I get out of a Queensland contract if the valuation is low? Possibly, if the contract is still subject to finance and you have taken reasonable steps to obtain it. Get legal advice before the finance date.
