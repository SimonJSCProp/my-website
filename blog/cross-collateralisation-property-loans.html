Cross-Collateralisation: Why Tying Your Properties to One Bank Can Trap You

4 min read
JSC Property Investments

Most investors never choose cross-collateralisation. It arrives quietly in a loan offer, often as the simplest way to use the equity in your home to buy an investment. The trouble usually shows up years later, when you want to sell, refinance or buy again, and discover the bank has a say over every property you own.

What is cross-collateralisation?

Cross-collateralisation is when one property secures more than one loan, so the lender relies on several properties for the combined debt (Duncan & Cooper, 2026). A common example is using your home and a new investment property together to secure all the borrowing for the purchase.

Separate loan accounts do not prove separate security. Two loans can sit in different accounts and still be secured by both properties, so the loan documents need checking (Vecchio, 2026).

Why do lenders like it?

It gives the lender more security and more control. Canstar notes that the lender gains greater control over all the properties involved, and that one property cannot be sold or refinanced without involving the others (Duncan & Cooper, 2026).

What happens when you sell one property?

This is where most investors feel the trap.

Before settlement, the lender must release its mortgage over the property being sold. If that property also secures other loans, the lender reviews the remaining debt and security first (Home Loan Experts, 2026). Your lender may require the sale proceeds to reduce the remaining loan balance (Catalyst, 2024).

Hunter Galloway’s worked example shows how much this can matter. A home and an investment property are crossed, with $1 million of total debt. The home’s value falls to $575,000 and the investment sells for $600,000. If the lender limits the remaining debt to 80% of the home’s value, it requires $540,000 from the sale, leaving the owner with about $60,000 in cash. With separate securities, the same sale leaves about $120,000 (Vecchio, 2026).

As Hunter Galloway puts it: “The lender decides what it needs repaid before releasing a property” (Vecchio, 2026).

Why is it riskier when values fall?

A lower valuation reduces the equity available for release and lifts your loan-to-value ratio if the debt stays the same (Home Loan Experts, 2026). When properties are crossed, a weak valuation on one can affect the lender’s view of all of them.

National home values have fallen for seven straight months (Cuthbert, 2026), so this is not a theoretical risk in 2026.

What else does it limit?

  • Refinancing. If you want to move only one loan, the existing lender must agree to release any security attached to it (Home Loan Experts, 2026).
  • Valuation costs. Lenders often need valuations for every property involved, which adds cost and may produce conservative figures (Catalyst, 2024).
  • Default. If you default, every property securing the debt can be enforced (Vecchio, 2026).
  • Future borrowing. With all your debt at one bank, that bank’s policy, including its own limits on high debt-to-income lending, decides your next step (Hermosilla, 2026).

How do you keep loans separate?

The usual alternative is a standalone structure. Instead of securing the new purchase with both properties, you take an equity release loan secured only by your home to fund the deposit and costs, then a separate loan secured only by the investment (Vecchio, 2026).

Separate securities do not reduce total debt. As Hunter Galloway notes, “Separate securities do not remove the consequences of falling property values” (Vecchio, 2026). What they do is keep each property’s decisions separate.

Can you untangle an existing cross-collateralised structure?

Usually, yes, but it costs time and money. The process generally involves reviewing your portfolio and serviceability, valuing each property, refinancing into standalone loans and registering new mortgages. One broker estimates a two-property restructure at about $1,500 to $3,000 in costs, and suggests timing it for the end of any fixed-rate period to avoid break costs (Hermosilla, 2026). Your current serviceability and valuations will decide whether it is possible.

Frequently asked questions

Is cross-collateralisation always bad? Not always, but it gives the lender more control. Most investors who plan to grow, sell or refinance benefit from keeping securities separate.

How do I know if my loans are cross-collateralised? Check your loan contracts and mortgage documents, or ask your lender which properties secure each loan.
